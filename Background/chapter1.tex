Multicategories are a generalization of categories. In 
categories, arrows have a single source/domain and a single target/codomain. In multicategories, the arrows have a finite sequence of sources/inputs and a single target/output. 
\begin{defn}
    A \emph{multicategory} $\M$ consist of a set $S$ of objects and for each $n\geq 0$ and each sequence $s_1,\ldots, s_n,t$ of objects a set
    \[\M(s_1,\ldots,s_n;t) \] 
    of multiarrows/operations, thought of as taking $n$ inputs objects $s_1,\ldots,s_n$ respectively to the output object $t$. Moreover, there are three kinds of structure maps:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For each object $s$, an identity/unit $1_s\in \M(s;s)$
        \item For any sequence of objects $s_1,\ldots,s_n,t$ and any $n$-tuple of sequences $d^i_1,\ldots,d^i_{k_i}$ of objects for $i=1,\ldots,n$, a composition function of multiarrows/operations 
        \[
        \gamma:\M(s_1,\ldots,s_n;t)\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\M(d^i_1,\ldots,d^i_{k_i};s_i)\rightarrow\M(d^1_1,\ldots,d^1_{k_1},d^2_1,\ldots,d^n_{k_n};t),
        \]
        usually written $p\circ(q_1,\ldots,q_n):=\gamma(p,q_1,\ldots,q_n)$ or even just $p(q_1,\ldots,q_n)$
    \end{itemize}
    Furthermore these structure maps have to satisfy a number of axioms, which express that composition is unital and associative \citep{HM}
    % {\bf ADD SPECIFIC CITATION WHERE THE FULL DEFINITION APPEARS.}
\end{defn}

\begin{rmk}
Let $\M$ be a multicategory, $f\in\M(s_1,\ldots,s_n;t)$ and $g\in\M(d_1,\ldots,d_l;s_i)$ for some $1 \leq i \leq n$. It will be convenient to have the following notation:
\[
\iof{f}{g} := f(1_{s_1},\ldots,g,\ldots,1_{s_n})
\]
Thus, $\iof{f}{g}$ is the composition of $f$ with $g$ in the $i$th variable.
\end{rmk}

\begin{ex}
    You can think of $n$-ary multiarrows in $\M(s_1,\ldots, s_n;t)$ as n-ary operations. Let $M$ be the multicategory with a single object $X$. Then the $n$-ary multiarrows in $M(X,\dots,X;X)$define to be the set of functions 
    \[
    f:X\times \cdots \times X \rightarrow X 
    \]
    where the domain is the n-fold cartesian product of $X$ with itself.
\end{ex}
\begin{ex} 
    We can up the level from the previous example, by fixing a family of sets $ \{X_c\}_{c\in C}$ indexed by a set of objects $C$. Then you can think of the multiarrows in $\M(c_1,\ldots,c_n;c)$ as n-ary operations, taking inputs of types $\lst{c}{n}$ respectively to an output of type $c$.

    For example, for a fixed family of sets $\{X_c\}_{c\in C}$, define a multicategory $\M$ whose objects is the set of colors $C$,  and whose multiarrows are defined by declaring $\M(c_1,\ldots, c_n;c)$ to be the set of functions 
     \[
     p:X_{c_1}\times \cdots \times X_{c_n}\rightarrow X_c
    \]
    Composition in $\M$ is defined as composition of functions.
\end{ex}

We can observe that multicategories form a category $\MCat$.


\begin{defn}
For two multicategories $\M$ and $\mathcal{N}$, a \emph{multifunctor} $\phi:\M\rightarrow\mathcal{N}$ consist of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A function $f:O_{\M}\rightarrow O_{\mathcal{N}}$ between the corresponding sets of objects
    \item For each sequence $s_1,\ldots,s_n,t$ of objects of $\M$, a map 
    \[
        \phi_{\lst{s}{n},t}:\M(\lst{s}{n};t)\rightarrow \mathcal{N}(\flst{s}{n}{f};f(t)).
    \]
\end{itemize}
So that these maps are compatible with compositions and units in the obvious way.
\end{defn}
\subsection{The Tensor Product of Multicategories}
\label{sec:The Tensor Product of Multicategories}

Let $M$ and $N$ be multicategories. Their Boardman-Vogt tensor product $M \bv N$ is a multicategory defined to make sense of what a $M$-algebra in the category of $N$-algebras is and viceversa. We will not talk about the algebras of a multicategories here, but we refer the reader to [look for reference] % citep{?} and \citep{?} %} for more details.
\begin{defn}
    The \emph{Boardman-Vogt tensor} $M \bv N$ in terms of generators and relations. The set of objects of this multicategory is the product of the sets of objects of $M$ and $N$; if $x$ and $y$ are objects of $M$ and $N$ respectively, we write $x\otimes y$ for the corresponding object of $M \bv N$. The generating multiarrows of $M \bv N$ are of two types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For each multiarrow $f \in M (\lst{x}{n},x)$ and object $y$ of $N$, a multiarrow \[
    f \otimes y \in (M\bv N)(\tflst{x}{n}{y};x\otimes y)
    \]
    \item For each object $x$ of $M$ and multiarrow $g \in N(\lst{y}{m};y)$, a multiarrow
    \[
    x \otimes g \in (M\bv N)(\lst{x\otimes y}{m}; x\otimes y)
    \]
\end{itemize}
The relations to be satisfied by these generators are the following:
\begin{enumerate}[label = (\arabic*)]
    \item $(f\otimes y)(\tflst{f}{n}{y}) = f(\lst{f}{n})\otimes y$
    \item $(x\otimes g)(\lst{x\otimes g}{m})= x \otimes g(\lst{g}{m})$
    \item $\sigma^*(f \otimes y) = (\sigma^* f)\otimes y$ for $\sigma \in \Sigma_n$
    \item $\sigma^*(x \otimes g) = x \otimes (\sigma^* g)$ for $\sigma \in \Sigma_m$
    \item $(f \otimes y)(\tflst{x}{n}{g}) = \sigma^*_{n,m}((x \otimes g)(\lst{f\otimes y}{m}))$
\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

Note that relations (1) and (2) say precisely the condition that for any object $y$ of $N$ there is a multifunction $-\otimes y:M \rightarrow M \bv N$. Similarly, for any object $x$ of $M$ there is a multifunction $x \otimes -:N \rightarrow M \bv N$, this time given by relations (2) and (4). Relation (5) is the most interesting; most often referred to it as the \emph{Boardman-Vogt interchange relation}. The permutation $\sigma_{n,m} \in \Sigma_{nm}$ is the one that makes sense of formula (5), i.e. the element relating the sequences $(\lst{x_1 \otimes y}{m},\dots,\lst{x_n \otimes y}{m})$ and $(\tflst{x}{n}{d_1},\dots,\tflst{x}{n}{d_m})$.

\subsection{Trees}
\label{sec:trees}
We will do a quick detour to talk about our main combinatorial object that is gonna help us with studying multicategories, trees. We are mainly interested in finite rooted trees. So we don;t get confused with the more general notion of tree in graph theory, we will give a precise definition of what we mean by a tree in this thesis. We will see that these trees give rise to free multicategories whose objects are the edges of the tree and morphisms are generated by the vertices of the tree.
\begin{defn}
    A \emph{tree} $T$ consist of a finite set $V$ of \emph{vertices}, a nonempty finite set $E$ of \emph{edges}, a distinguished element $r\in E$ called the \emph{root}, together with the following data:
    \begin{enumerate}[label= (\arabic*)]
        \item a function $I:E-\{r\}\rightarrow V$, which we think of as assigning to an edge $e$ the vertex $I(e)$ of which is an input. The way to think about this function is that "$I(e)=f$ if and only if $e$ is \emph{an} input edge of $f$"
        \item A function $O:V\rightarrow E$, assigning to each vertex $g$ its output edge $O(g)$. The way to think about this function is that "$O(f)=x$ if and only if $x$ is \emph{the} output edge of $f$"
    \end{enumerate}
For each edge $e$ other than the root $r$, we obtain a sequence of edges starting at $e$ by repeatedly applying $O\circ I$. We demand that this sequence ends in the root $r$ after finitely many steps, for an arbitrary starting edge.
\end{defn}

As we can see the main difference between our definition of a tree and the usual definition of a tree in graph theory is that we allow for "orphan"/"leaf" edges that are not attached to any vertex as an output edge, and a unique designed edge that will not be attached to any vertex as an input edge (the root). This special edge also gives us a natural orientation of the tree, where we think of the root as the bottom and the leaves as the top, and thus a partial ordering on both the set of edges and the set of vertices.

Now I will introduce some terminology to talk about the different parts of a tree. The edges in the complement of the image of $O$ are called \emph{leaves} of the tree $T$. The vertices in the complement of the image of $I$ are called \emph{stumps}, or \emph{nullary vertices.} An \emph{outer edge} is an edge that is either the root or a leaf. An \emph{inner edge} is any other edge of $T$. Such an edge is both an output edge and an input edge for some other vertex. The vertices come in two kinds, \emph{external vertices} which only have one inner edge attached to them, and \emph{internal vertices} 
When writing $T$ for a tree, we will usually write $E(T)$ and $V(T)$ for its sets of edges and vertices, respectively.

\begin{defn}
    An \emph{embedding} $\psi:S\rightarrow T$ of trees consists of injective maps $\psi_V:V(S)\rightarrow V(T)$ and $\psi_E:E(S)\rightarrow E(T)$ such that for any vertex $f$ of $S$, the map $\psi_E$ gives a bijection between $in(f)$ and $in(\psi_V(f))$, and $\psi_E(O(f))=O(\psi_V(f))$. If the an embedding is bijective on both edges and vertices, then it is an isomorphism of trees.
\end{defn} 

If an embedding of trees, $\iota:S\rightarrow T$ is given by inclusion maps of $E(S)\subseteq E(T)$ and $V(S)\subseteq V(T)$, we will say that $S$ is a \emph{subtree} of $T$. Then we can see that any embedding $\psi:S\rightarrow T$ factors as an isomorphism followed by the inclusion of a subtree.

A tree $T$ defines a multicategory $\Omega(T)$ as follows: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The objects of $\Omega(T)$ are the edges of $T$.
    \item $\Omega(T)(e_1,\ldots,e_n;e)$ is either empty or a singleton if there exist a subtree $C_n$ with leaves $\lst{e}{n}$ and root $e$.  
    \item Composition is given by the tree structure in the obvious way. Expanding the details of it, we get the composition operation/multiarrow by identifying the root of the precomposing operation with a leave of the postcomposing operation and squashing this new inner edge and identifying the two vertices to the composition of the two corresponding operations/multiarrows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Category of trees $\Omega$}
\label{sec:dset}

Dendroidal sets are a generalization of simplicial sets that are particularly well suited to study operads/multicategories and multicategories. Just as simplicial sets are presheaves on the simplex category $\Delta$, dendroidal sets are presheaves on the dendroidal category $\Omega$.
\begin{defn}
    The category $\Omega$ consist of:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Objects: finite rooted trees
        \item Morphisms: for two trees $S$ and $T$, a morphism from $S$ to $T$ is a multifunctor $\Omega(S)\rightarrow \Omega(T)$ between the corresponding multicategories.
    \end{enumerate}
    Another way to say this is that $\Omega$ is the full subcategory of $\MCat$ spanned by the multicategories of the form $\Omega(T)$ for some tree $T$.
\end{defn}
 
The linear trees $\left[n\right]$ define a full subcategory of $\Omega$ isomorphic to the simplex category $\Delta$. Thus, there is an inclusion functor $i : \Delta \rightarrow \Omega$. This inclusion is compatible with the inclusion of $\cat{Cat}$ into $\MCat$ in the sense that the following diagram commutes:

\subsection{Faces and Degeneracies in $\Omega$}
\label{sec:faces and degeneracies}
As we know, the simplex category $\Delta$ has two important families of morphisms, the face maps and the degeneracy maps. These maps generate all other morphisms in $\Delta$. Good news for $\Omega$ being some multicategorical extension of $\Delta$ is that it also has two families of morphisms that generate all other morphisms in $\Omega$.

\subsubsection{Inner Faces}
For a tree $T$ and an inner edge $x$, let $\partial_x T$ denote the face of $T$ obtained by contracting the edge $x$, and identifying the vertices $f$ and $g$ at either end of $x$. This is an \emph{inner face} of $T$. This gives an inclusion of trees denoted
\[
\partial_xT\xrightarrow{\delta_x} T
\]
which sends the new vertex obtained by contraction to the subtree of T obtained by grafting the two corollas with vertices $f$ and $g$ along the edge $x$, i.e. the smallest subtree of $T$ containing these vertices. We can see these in terms of their associated operads. In this framework, we see that we send the new vertex in $\partial_eT$ to the composition in $\Omega(T)$ of the operations correspondingly given by $f$ and $g$ along $x$. A morphism of these kind will be referred to as an \emph{inner face}

\subsubsection{Degeneracies}

Let $x$ be an edge in a tree $T$. Consider the tree $\sigma_eT$ obtained from $T$ by splitting this edge into two edges $x_1$ and $x_2$ by placing an unary vertex $id_x$ in the middle of $x$. The  there is a morphism \[\sigma_xT\xrightarrow{\sigma_e} T \]
in $\Omega$ which sends both edges $x_1$ and $x_2$ to $x$ and the new unary vertex $id_x$ to the subtree $\mu \xrightarrow{x} T$ of $T$. In the operadic framework, we see that it sends the new vertex $id_x$ to the identity operation of the color $x$ in $\Omega(T)$. A morphism of this kind is called a \emph{degeneracy}.
 
As with simplices, we have codendroidal identities. We won't go over them since we won't be using them in any kind of way. To see a full list and more on them please refer to \cite[\S 3.3.4]{HM}. Following suit from simplices, we can also prove that any morphism of trees can be factorized into a composition of a degeneracy, followed by an isomorphism and ending with a face map.

\subsection{Dendroidal Sets}
\label{Dendroidal set}
As no surprise to the reader, the definition of a \emph{dendroidal set} is analogous to the definition of a simplicial set.
\begin{defn}
    A dendroidal set is a functor 
    \[X\ :\ \Omega^{\op} \xrightarrow{} \Set  \]
    Natural transformations as morphism between them, makes them form the category of dendroidal set, $\dset$
\end{defn}

Expounding out the definition of a dendroidal sets, we can see a dendroidal set is given by a family of sets $X_T$, one for each $T\in \Omega$, together with a map 
\[\alpha^*\ : \ X_T \rightarrow X_S \]
for every morphism $\alpha : S \rightarrow t$ in $\Omega$. These maps should be functorial. The elements of $X_T$ are called \emph{dendrices} of $X$ of shape $T$, or simply $T$-dendrices of $X$.

In particular, each set $X_T$ carries a n action of the group $Aut(T )$ and the dendroidal set $X$ is completely determined by the collection of all these $Aut(T)$-sets together with the elementary face and degeneracy operators of the three kinds we saw before, satisfying the \emph{dendroidal identities}. dual to the identities previously mentioned.

\begin{ex}
    Any tree $T$ of $\Omega$ gives rise to a representable presheaf which we denote by $\Omega[T]$. As in any presheaf category this are defined by
    \[\Omega[T]_S = \Hom_\Omega(S,T) \]
\end{ex}

\begin{ex}
    One can from limits and colimits of diagrams of dendroidal sets by calculating them `pointwise', as in any presheaf category. For instance, we can from the product $X\times Y $ of dendroidal sets $X$ and $Y $ as 
    \[(X \times Y )_T = X_T \times Y_T  \]   
\end{ex}

\begin{ex}
    In particular, each face $\delta_x : \partial_x T \rightarrow   T   $, where $x$ is an inner edge or outer vertex, defines a monomorphism of dendroidal sets
    \[\Omega[\partial_x T] \rightarrow \Omega[T ] \].
    We will write $\partial_c\Omega[T]\subseteq\Omega[T]$ for the image of this morphism and call it the \emph{face of $T$ opposite $c$}. The union of all these (inner and outer faces) defines the \emph{boundary} $\partial\Omega[T]$ of $\Omega[T]$. Note that these faces and the boundary can be explicitly described as follows:
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
            (\partial_c\Omega[T])_S &= \{\alpha:S\rightarrow T\ |\ \text{the edge } c \text{ is not contained in the image of } \alpha\},\\
            (\partial_f\Omega[T])_S &=\{\alpha:S\rightarrow T\ |\ \text{no subtree } \alpha(g) \text{ contains the vertex } f\},\\
            (\partial\Omega[T])_S &=\{\alpha : S \rightarrow T\ |\ \alpha \text{ factors through a proper inclusion } R \rightarrow T \}.
        \end{aligned}
    \]

\end{ex}

\begin{ex}
    The category $\Omega$ has been defined as a full subcategory of the category in $\Set$. Thus any such operad $\boldsymbol{M}$ defines a dendroidal set $\textup{N}\boldsymbol{M}$, its \emph{dendroidal nerve}, by 
    \[\textup{N}\boldsymbol{M}_T = \boldsymbol{Op}(\Omega(T), \boldsymbol{M}). \]
    Even though this is an standard construction in homotopical category theory, let us take a closer look at the dendroidal set given by the operad. For the tree $\mu$, the set $\textup{N}\boldsymbol{M}_\mu$ is the set of colors of $\boldsymbol{M}$.For the n-corolla $C_n$ and a sequence of colors $(\lst{c}{n};c)$ a sequence of colors of $\boldsymbol{M}$, there is a pullback square 
    \textbf{PUT PULLBACK SQUARE}
    Here the right vertical map has as its $i^{th}$ component the map $i^* : \bnerv{M}_{C_n} \rightarrow \bnerv{M}_\mu$, induced by the edge inclusion $i : \mu \rightarrow C_n$, while $\boldsymbol{c}$ denotes the sequence $(\lst{c}{n};c)$. More generally, for a tree $T$ a dendrex in $\bnerv{M}_T$ can be pictured as follows. It consist of an assignments $x$ of colors of $\boldsymbol{M}$ to the edges of $T$ and a equivalence class of pairs $(p,f)$, where $p $ is a planar structure on $T $ and $f $ assigns an operation $f(v)\in\boldsymbol{M}(\flst{e}{n}{c};c(e))$ to each vertex $v\in T$, where $e $ denotes the output edge of $v $ and $\lst{e }{n }$ enumerates the input edges of $v $ in the order provided by the planar structure $p $. Two such pairs $(p,f)$ and $(q,g)$ are equivalent if for each vertex $v$, the permutation $\sigma$ relating the planar structure $p $ and $q $ satisfies $f(v)=\sigma^* g(v)$.

    A little more informally, a dendrex in $\bnerv{M}_T$ is an assignment of colors of $\boldsymbol{M}$ to the edges of $T  $ and a compatible assignment of an operation in $\boldsymbol{M}$ to each vertex of $T$. As explained above, this is only a precise statement if one has a planar structure on $T$ in minds.
\end{ex}
